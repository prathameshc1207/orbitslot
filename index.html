Hardware ground station

Connect an ESP32 with an OLED, LED, buzzer and MPU6050 over USB. It mirrors one station from this dashboard, and you can break that station or trigger an emergency by handling the board. Works in Chrome or Edge on a laptop, on your own deployed copy of this page.

Not connected
  • OLED shows the station's live status: time, which satellite it is downlinking, priority, progress, next pass, urgent and missed counts.
  • LED is on during a downlink, flickers for high-priority data, and blinks slowly when the station is offline.
  • Buzzer chirps when a downlink starts, beeps three times when urgent requests arrive, and sounds low when a deadline is missed or the station fails.
  • MPU6050 is the antenna sensor. Turn the board face-down for a second to knock the station offline; turn it back up to restore it. Shake it to send six urgent requests. Tilting it shows antenna elevation against the elevation the satellite needs.

How it works

Real orbits. On the deployed site, a serverless function fetches the latest Planet TLEs from CelesTrak and the dashboard picks up to 24 real SuperDoves, propagated with SGP4 (satellite.js) from the current minute. Without that connection it falls back to a built-in snapshot: three real TLEs (Flock 4G, 4H and 4BE, epoch 8 October 2026) each spread into eight satellites around the same orbit. Passes are the times a satellite is above the minimum elevation at each station.

The data. Each satellite has an assumed downlink rate between 120 and 380 Mbps and a queue of requests with a size, priority, arrival time and deadline. Requests are generated from the seed so runs can be reproduced.

Shared rules. A station tracks one satellite at a time and needs the re-pointing time between different satellites. A satellite talks to one station at a time. Contacts shorter than the minimum are not allowed. Offline stations cannot be used.

FCFS. Passes are served in the order satellites come overhead. Each satellite sends its oldest request first.

Priority-first. Whenever a station is free, it serves the overhead satellite with the most urgent data waiting at that moment, and sends urgent data first. It sorts by priority but never looks ahead.

OrbitSlot. It plans the whole day. It books, step by step, the contact that adds the most value (weight × GB delivered on time, plus 20% credit for late data), knowing each satellite's future passes and deadlines. A ruin-and-recreate search then clears random stretches of the timeline, rebuilds them, and keeps improvements. At the start of the day it also tries the other two plans as starting points, so its first plan never scores below theirs.

Replanning. Failures and surprise requests are not known in advance. When one happens, the past is locked, a contact cut off by a failure ends there, and every scheduler rebuilds only the future. OrbitSlot starts from its previous plan, so it changes only what it needs to.

Decisions you can inspect. Clicking a contact shows what it sent, which satellites were competing, what each was waiting to send, and for OrbitSlot how much score the alternative would have added.
